Lesson 1

Every great project starts with a great question.

A research question is the one sentence your whole project is built to answer. Get it right and everything else (what data to collect, how to analyze it, what to write) falls into place. Get it wrong and you'll be stuck researching "everything about social media" forever.

Step 1

First, pick your type of question

Most research falls into two families. The type of question you ask decides the kind of data you'll collect.

QUALITATIVE
Ask "How…?"

Explores experiences, meanings, and processes. You want to understand what something is like from people's point of view.

Data
Words: interviews, open-ended survey answers, social media posts, observations
Answer
Themes and patterns, backed by quotes
Sample
Smaller and deeper (e.g., 8–30 people)
"How do high school students decide what to post on their Instagram 'Close Friends' story versus their main feed?"
QUANTITATIVE
Ask a yes/no question

Tests whether there's a relationship or difference between things you can measure with numbers.

Data
Numbers: surveys with rating scales, screen-time logs, test scores, experiments
Answer
Yes / no / how strongly, backed by statistics
Sample
Larger and broader (e.g., 50+ people)
"Does scrolling TikTok for more than an hour before bed correlate with fewer hours of sleep among high school juniors?"
💡

Grad-school shortcut: If you're asking "how", you're probably doing qualitative research. If your question can be answered "yes" or "no" (Does…? Is…? Do…?), you're probably doing quantitative research.

It's a rule of thumb, not a law. Some qualitative questions start with "What" or "Why," and some quantitative ones ask "How much." But it's the quickest way to check that your question and your method match.

Step 2

Six checks for a strong question

Let's sharpen one idea: "mental health on TikTok." Tap each card to see how it improves the question.

Put it all together

"How do TikTok creators and commenters express experiences of anxiety in the most-viewed #mentalhealth videos posted during Mental Health Awareness Month (May 2026)?"

It starts with "How" and the data is posts and comments, so this is a QUALITATIVE question. Next step: how would you actually collect that data?

Step 3

"I want to study a trend on social media… but how do I measure it?"

You don't need to survey anyone to research social media. Public posts and comments can be your data. The trick is deciding exactly which posts before you start, so you're not just scrolling.

  1. Step 1

    Pick your keywords

    Choose 1–3 hashtags and write them down before you search.

    #mentalhealth, #anxiety
  2. Step 2

    Set a time window

    Only include posts from a set date range. Record the day you collected data, because TikTok changes daily.

    Posted May 1–31, 2026 · collected June 7
  3. Step 3

    Choose your sample

    Set a rule for which videos and comments count, and stick to it.

    10 most-viewed videos × 50 most-liked comments each
  4. Step 4

    Save it in a spreadsheet

    One row per comment: video link, views, likes, and the comment paraphrased in your own words.

    ~500 rows
  5. Step 5

    Code it

    Read everything once. Then tag each comment with short labels ("codes") for the ideas you see over and over.

    humor as coping · self-diagnosis · asking for help
  6. Step 6

    Find themes

    Group related codes into 3–5 big themes. Count how often each appears, and pick a few examples for each one. This is thematic analysis.

    Theme: "Laughing so I don't cry"

⚠️ Watch out: common traps

  • "Trend" means change over time. One month of posts gives you a snapshot. To claim a trend, compare two time windows (for example, May 2025 vs. May 2026).
  • The algorithm is personal. TikTok shows you results based on your history. Search from a new account if you can, and mention this as a limitation.
  • "Most viewed" means most visible, not typical. That's a fine choice. Just describe it accurately, and don't claim you studied "what TikTok users think."
  • Start smaller than you think. 10 videos × 100 comments = 1,000 comments to read and code by hand. 50 comments per video is plenty for a first project.
  • Protect people, even on public posts. Don't include usernames. Paraphrase comments so nobody can search them and find the person. Skip accounts that look like they belong to minors.

From a real published study

Researchers interviewed students your age

In a 2024 study published in the journal Global Media and China, a research team from Columbia University asked:

How do middle school and high school students in China engage with news content on short video platforms like Douyin?

Lei, Y., Meng, X., Huang, X., Li, J., Jiang, Y., Yu, Y., & Literat, I. (2024). Read the paper

Question starts with "How"
→ it's qualitative
So the method was interviews
→ 30 students, ages 13–18, from five provinces, over Zoom
So the results were themes
→ students valued objective news, had their own fact-checking habits, and preferred discussing news offline with people they trust

    The anatomy of a research paper

    Almost every research paper in psychology, education, and the social sciences follows the same structure. Once you know it, you can read any paper faster and write your own with a clear map.

    ⏳

    Think of it as an hourglass. A paper starts wide (why this topic matters to the world), narrows to your specific study (what exactly you did and found), then widens again (what it means for the world). Click any section to explore it.

    The yellow running example is a made-up student study. The teal box shows how a real published paper did it.

    Pro tip

    Researchers don't read papers front to back

    When you're doing your literature review, you'll skim dozens of papers. Here's the order experienced researchers use to decide quickly whether a paper is useful:

    1 · Title & Abstract→ 2 · Discussion / Conclusion→ 3 · Tables & figures→ 4 · Method→ 5 · Everything else, if it's a keeper
